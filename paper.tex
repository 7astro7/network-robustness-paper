\begin{abstract}
Power-law networks tolerate extensive random failures yet collapse abruptly under targeted
attacks, often with little warning when monitored only through connectivity measures such
as the giant connected component (GCC). We ask whether information-theoretic rates of
structural change can provide early-warning signals of fragmentation under progressive
node removal.

We analyze synthetic scale-free networks generated by the Chung--Lu model with degree
exponents $2<\gamma<3$ under random failure and targeted (hub-first) removal. At each
increment we compute the successive Kullback--Leibler divergence between adjacent
empirical degree distributions, yielding a signal that quantifies the instantaneous rate
of topological reorganization. Using fixed smoothing and pre-specified decision rules, we
find that under random failure the signal departs from an initial stationary baseline
well before GCC collapse across a sweep of $\gamma$, producing nontrivial warning lead time.
Under targeted removal, structural change begins immediately and collapse can occur before
any trigger; here the same signal serves primarily as a measure of disruption intensity.
We additionally validate the random-failure behavior on an empirical Internet topology
graph from the CAIDA AS Relationships dataset.

Overall, successive KL divergence provides a robust and interpretable rate-based indicator
of structural degradation that complements classical percolation-based robustness analysis.
\end{abstract}


\section*{Introduction and Motivation}
Many real-world communication, social, and biological networks are known to exhibit
heavy-tailed degree distributions~\cite{newman2003structure}.

For graphs $G=(V,E)$ with degree counts $\{k_i\}$, one often observes
\[
P(k) \propto k^{-\gamma}, \qquad \gamma \in (2,3),
\]
reflecting extreme heterogeneity and the presence of high-degree hubs.
Network robustness is traditionally evaluated using the giant connected component (GCC) fraction $S(q)$ under random or targeted node removal. However, this connectivity-based perspective provides limited insight into how topological structure degrades prior to global fragmentation. As emphasized in recent reviews, robustness transitions in complex networks often occur abruptly and may exhibit little to no reliable early-warning signals when assessed using classical indicators such as the GCC \cite{radicchi2024robustness}. This lack of anticipatory indicators poses significant challenges for monitoring the stability of real-world networks. A complementary line of work studies early-warning signals in dynamical processes
on networks, where instability is approached through changes in node-state statistics
rather than through structural damage. For example, Suweis and D'Odorico~\cite{suweis2014earlywarning}
analyze multivariate stochastic dynamics on interaction networks and show that rising
variance and cross-correlation can anticipate loss of stability in the underlying
networked system. Our setting differs in that we consider progressive structural
degradation under node removal, where no node-state time series is assumed; we therefore
seek early warning directly from topological reorganization, using successive KL
divergence between degree distributions as a purely structural signal.


Information-theoretic approaches offer a complementary perspective to connectivity-based robustness measures by quantifying distributional change rather than component-level size.
In particular, the Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence between degree distributions at successive damage levels captures rates of structural reorganization and may reveal early signs of impending network collapse. The central question of this work is:

\begin{quote}
Can information-theoretic rates of structural change in the degree distribution provide early-warning signals of impending connectivity collapse in power-law networks under node removal?
\end{quote}

Although information-theoretic quantities have been used to characterize static graph structure, their behavior under progressive damage remains largely unexplored. Dehmer~(2008)~\cite{dehmer2008information} introduced Shannon-entropy measures to quantify graph complexity, and subsequent work has examined entropy-based summaries of degree heterogeneity or structural diversity. However, these studies focus on fixed network topologies and do not connect entropy to dynamical processes such as node failures, percolation transitions, or GCC collapse.
Likewise, the extensive literature on network robustness—ranging from classical results on error and attack tolerance in scale-free graphs \cite{albert2000error,cohen2000resilience} to modern treatments of resilience \cite{radicchi2024robustness}—relies primarily on quantities such as degree moments, spectral radius, or percolation thresholds. Very little attention is given to information-theoretic indicators that might change before observable topological collapse.

Unlike entropy, which summarizes a distribution at a fixed damage level, KL divergence enables comparison of successive states and is therefore sensitive to rates of structural change. The KL divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q)$ quantifies the information-theoretic distance between two probability distributions, measuring how much additional information is required to encode samples from $P$ using a code optimized for $Q$. When applied to successive degree distributions $P_{q+\Delta q}$ and $P_q$, this quantity captures the instantaneous rate at which the network's structural organization is changing as damage accumulates. Unlike moment-based statistics such as mean degree or degree variance, KL divergence is sensitive to the full shape of the distribution and can detect subtle shifts in connectivity patterns before they manifest as macroscopic changes. The asymmetric nature of KL divergence provides a temporal signal that naturally captures directional changes in structural degradation.

To our knowledge, no prior work has systematically tracked the KL divergence of the degree distribution as a function of node-removal probability, nor evaluated whether KL divergence exhibits predictive signatures prior to critical fragmentation points. This gap motivates our investigation of $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ as a potential early-warning signal of structural failure in power-law networks.

\section*{Problem Definition}

Let $G_0$ denote an undamaged network with degree distribution $P_0(k)$. Consider independent node failures with probability $q$, producing a damaged graph $G_q$ with observed distribution $P_q(k)$. Define the successive Kullback--Leibler divergence between degree distributions at adjacent damage levels:
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{q+\Delta q} \| P_q) = \sum_k P_{q+\Delta q}(k) \log_2 \frac{P_{q+\Delta q}(k)}{P_q(k)}.
\]

We seek to characterize the functional relationship between $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ and:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
    \item the GCC fraction $S(q)$,
    \item the critical failure probability $q_c$,
    \item the shape and abruptness of the $S(q)$ transition,
    \item robustness under random vs.\ degree-biased attacks.
\end{itemize}

Our goal is not to modify the network locally, but to evaluate how global, information-theoretic measures of distributional change reflect the onset of fragmentation.

\subsection*{Theoretical Background}

For uncorrelated random graphs, let $k$ denote the degree of a randomly chosen node, with
$\langle k \rangle$ and $\langle k^2 \rangle$ denoting the first and second moments of the
degree distribution. The Molloy--Reed criterion states that a giant connected component
exists when
\[
\kappa = \frac{\langle k^2 \rangle - \langle k \rangle}{\langle k \rangle} > 1.
\]
Under independent node failures with probability $q$, this condition yields an
approximate critical threshold
\[
(1 - q)\kappa \approx 1
\qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
q_c \approx 1 - \frac{\langle k \rangle}{\langle k^2 \rangle - \langle k \rangle}.
\]

This framework highlights that classical percolation theory characterizes collapse in
terms of low-order moments of the degree distribution. However, many distinct degree
distributions can share the same values of $\langle k \rangle$ and $\langle k^2 \rangle$,
particularly in heterogeneous networks. As a result, substantial redistribution of
connectivity can occur while moment-based quantities change only gradually.

Progressive node removal induces continuous reshaping of the degree distribution well
before the percolation threshold is reached. Under random failure, high-degree nodes are
removed infrequently at early stages, leading to slow erosion of the tail, whereas
targeted removal produces immediate and asymmetric distortion of the distribution. These
structural changes precede macroscopic fragmentation and are not directly captured by
connectivity-based criteria.

Although KL divergence does not appear explicitly in the Molloy--Reed condition, it is
sensitive to such redistribution because it compares the full degree distributions at
successive damage levels. Our objective is therefore not to predict the collapse point
itself, but to detect the onset of structural reorganization that ultimately leads to
abrupt fragmentation, an issue emphasized in recent reviews \cite{radicchi2024robustness}.

\section*{Hypotheses}

Classical analyses of network robustness emphasize connectivity-based quantities such as
the size of the giant connected component and the location of percolation thresholds.
While these measures characterize the point of global failure, they provide limited
information about how structural degradation unfolds prior to collapse. In this work,
we instead focus on the rate of structural change in the network's degree
distribution as damage accumulates.

We formulate the following hypotheses regarding the behavior of information-theoretic
change rates under different node-removal regimes, along with explicit empirical
predictions.

\subsection*{H1: Stationarity under Random Failure}

Under random node removal, scale-free networks exhibit an initial regime in which
structural changes are small and approximately stationary. We hypothesize that, at low
damage levels, the rate of change in the degree distribution fluctuates weakly around a
stable baseline, reflecting the network’s tolerance to random failure.

As damage accumulates, this stationarity is eventually lost prior to macroscopic
fragmentation.

\paragraph{Predictions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The successive KL divergence remains approximately constant for small values
    of $q$, forming a well-defined baseline.
    \item A statistically detectable deviation from this baseline occurs at a damage
    level $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$ strictly less than the collapse point
    $q_{\mathrm{collapse}}$.
    \item The warning point $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$ exhibits moderate variability across
    repeated runs with different random seeds.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{H2: Immediate Nonstationarity under Targeted Failure}

Under targeted (hub-first) node removal, structural degradation begins immediately.
Because high-degree nodes are removed first, the degree distribution is altered
substantially even at small damage fractions. We hypothesize that, in this regime, no
initial stationary baseline exists for the rate of structural change.

Instead, structural degradation is characterized by immediate nonstationarity in the rate
of change, with large early deviations that may not evolve monotonically.

\paragraph{Predictions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The successive KL divergence does not exhibit a stationary baseline at low values of $q$.
    \item The signal shows a pronounced early deviation caused by immediate removal of high-degree nodes.
    \item A drift-based trigger may not occur before collapse; in this case the signal is interpreted as a disruption-intensity measure rather than an advance-warning point.
\end{itemize}


\subsection*{H3: Robustness across the Power-Law Regime}

The qualitative behaviors described in H1 and H2 are hypothesized to persist across
scale-free networks with degree exponents in the range
\[
2 < \gamma < 3.
\]

\paragraph{Predictions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item For all values of $\gamma$ in this range, an early-warning point
    $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$ can be identified using the same information-theoretic signal.
    \item The relative ordering $q_{\mathrm{warn}} < q_{\mathrm{collapse}}$ holds
    consistently across $\gamma$.
    \item While the mean value of $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$ may vary with $\gamma$, the
    existence of an early-warning regime is robust to changes in degree heterogeneity.
\end{itemize}


\section*{Metrics}

To evaluate the hypotheses outlined previously we require a metric that captures
how rapidly network structure changes as node removal progresses, rather than
the absolute level of connectivity or heterogeneity at a fixed damage level. All
metrics in this work are derived from the evolution of the degree distribution under
progressive node removal.

\subsection*{Degree Distribution as Structural State}

At each damage fraction $q$, we compute the empirical degree distribution
\[
P_q(k) = \frac{1}{|V(G_q)|} \sum_{v \in V(G_q)} \mathbf{1}\{\deg(v) = k\},
\]
where $G_q$ denotes the graph after a fraction $q$ of nodes has been removed.
The degree distribution serves as a compact representation of the network’s
structural state, encoding changes in connectivity patterns as damage accumulates.

\subsection*{Successive Kullback--Leibler Divergence}

To quantify incremental structural change, we compute the successive
Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence between degree distributions at adjacent damage levels:
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P_{q+\Delta q} \,\|\, P_q\right)
=
\sum_k
P_{q+\Delta q}(k)
\log_2
\frac{P_{q+\Delta q}(k)}{P_q(k)}, 
\]
where $\Delta q$ denotes the removal increment between successive damage levels (here $\Delta q = 0.9/99$ since we evaluate 100 evenly spaced values of $q \in [0,0.9]$).

Unlike KL divergence relative to a fixed reference distribution, the successive form
measures the instantaneous rate at which the network’s degree structure changes as
damage increases. Large values indicate abrupt reorganization of connectivity patterns,
while small values correspond to gradual or stationary structural evolution.
For reference, we also define the degree entropy at damage level $q$ as
\[
H(q) = -\sum_k P_q(k)\log_2 P_q(k),
\]
which summarizes degree heterogeneity at a fixed structural state.

While degree entropy $H(q)$ summarizes heterogeneity of the degree distribution at a fixed damage level,
successive Kullback--Leibler divergence explicitly encodes \emph{change} between successive states of damage.
Consequently, KL divergence can be sensitive to structural reorganization even in regimes where entropy evolves gradually, motivating its use as a complementary indicator for early warning.

\paragraph{Support and numerical stability.}
Because $P_q(k)$ is estimated empirically from a finite graph, some degree values may attain zero observed probability at intermediate damage levels. To ensure $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{q+\Delta q}\,\|\,P_q)$ is well-defined across all $q$, we evaluate degree distributions on a fixed discrete support for each run and apply a small additive regularization $P_q(k)\leftarrow P_q(k)+\varepsilon$ before normalization, with $\varepsilon = 10^{-12}$.

\subsection*{Signal Smoothing}

The raw successive KL divergence may exhibit high-frequency fluctuations arising from
finite-size effects and stochastic variability in node removal. To suppress such noise
while preserving trend information, we apply an exponentially weighted moving average
(EWMA) to the successive KL sequence:
\[
\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_i)
=
\alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_i)
+
(1-\alpha)\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{i-1}).
\]

The smoothing parameter is fixed at $\alpha = 0.2$ across all experiments. 
A brief sensitivity check indicates that the detected warning point can shift with $\alpha$, particularly under targeted removal where the signal is nonstationary and the drift-based rule is sensitive to short-run fluctuations. Accordingly, we fix $\alpha=0.2$ as a moderate smoothing choice and report results using this single pre-specified value rather than selecting $\alpha$ post hoc.
Although the update rule depends only on the previous smoothed value, the EWMA 
assigns exponentially decaying weight to past observations, 
corresponding to an effective window
length of approximately $1/\alpha$. No optimization over $\alpha$ is performed.

The smoothed signal $\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q)$ is the sole early-warning observable used
throughout this study.


\subsection*{Early-Warning Decision Rules}

Because the same structural signal may exhibit qualitatively different behavior under
different failure regimes, early warning is identified using regime-appropriate decision
rules applied to $\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q)$.

\paragraph{Random Failure.}
Under random node removal, early warning is identified by detecting the loss of an
initial low-activity regime in which $\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q)$ fluctuates weakly around
a baseline. We define a baseline window $q \le 0.15$ and compute the baseline mean $\mu_0$ and standard deviation
$\sigma_0$ over this range. The warning point $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$ is defined as the
smallest $q$ such that
\[
\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q) > \mu_0 + 2\sigma_0.
\]

\paragraph{Targeted Failure.}
Under targeted (hub-first) removal, structural change begins immediately and no stationary
baseline exists. Because the successive KL signal compares adjacent damage levels, it is
naturally indexed on the midpoint grid
\[
q_{i+\frac12}=\frac{q_i+q_{i+1}}{2}.
\]
We define the warning point $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$ as the smallest midpoint $q_{i+\frac12}$
with $q_{i+\frac12}\ge q_0$ such that the smoothed successive KL divergence exhibits
persistent positive drift over a window of $m$ increments. Operationally, we require a
net increase over $m$ successive increments,
\[
\tilde D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{i+\frac12+m})-\tilde D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{i+\frac12})>0,
\]
while allowing up to a small number of local violations of strict monotonicity within the
window to reduce sensitivity to single-step noise. Throughout this study we use
$q_0=0.15$ and $m=3$.

In both regimes, the warning logic operates solely on the successive KL signal; the
difference lies only in the decision rule used to interpret that signal.

\section*{Experimental Design}

Our experimental design isolates how information-theoretic change rates behave under
progressive network damage across different structural regimes. We vary (i) the degree
heterogeneity of the underlying network and (ii) the node-removal mechanism, while
holding all detection logic fixed. For each configuration, we measure the separation
between an information-theoretic warning point and the onset of macroscopic connectivity
collapse.

\subsection*{Graph Model}

All networks studied in this work are generated using the Chung--Lu expected-degree
model with power-law degree sequences. This choice isolates the role of degree
heterogeneity while avoiding degree--degree correlations and growth effects inherent to
other generative mechanisms.

While other scale-free constructions such as preferential attachment or configuration
models are widely used, we restrict attention here to the Chung--Lu ensemble to ensure
that observed effects arise from distributional change rather than model-specific
correlations.

\subsection*{Failure Regimes}

We consider two node-removal regimes: (i) random failure, in which nodes are removed
uniformly at random, and (ii) targeted failure, in which nodes are removed in descending
order of degree (hub-first removal). In both cases, node removal proceeds incrementally
as a function of the removal fraction $q$, and all observables are evaluated on the
resulting damaged graph $G_q$.


\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Network Model}
We study robustness in synthetic scale-free networks with power-law degree distributions
\[
P(k) \propto k^{-\gamma},
\qquad 2 < \gamma < 3,
\]
a regime characteristic of many empirical infrastructure and communication networks.
Unless otherwise stated, all networks contain $N = 10{,}000$ nodes.

To examine sensitivity to degree heterogeneity, we sweep
\[
\gamma \in \{2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, 2.5, 2.6, 2.7, 2.8, 2.9\}.
\]
For each value of $\gamma$, results are averaged across multiple random seeds to account
for stochastic variability in network realization and node removal.

\subsection*{Failure Models}
The two failure regimes introduced in the Experimental Design section are implemented as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Random failure: nodes are removed uniformly at random.
    \item Targeted failure: nodes are removed in descending order of degree (hub-first removal).
\end{enumerate}

For each failure mode, a fraction $q \in [0, 0.9]$ of nodes is progressively removed.
All structural observables are computed on the resulting damaged graph $G_q$.

\subsection*{Structural Observables}
At each damage level $q$, we compute:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The giant connected component fraction
    \[
    S(q) = \frac{| \mathrm{GCC}(G_q) |}{|V(G_q)|},
    \]
    used only to contextualize collapse.
    \item The empirical degree distribution $P_q(k)$ of $G_q$.
\end{itemize}

No connectivity-based quantity is used in the early-warning logic.

\subsection*{Successive KL Divergence}
To quantify incremental structural change, we compute the successive
Kullback--Leibler divergence between degree distributions at adjacent damage levels:
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{q+\Delta q} \| P_q)
=
\sum_k
P_{q+\Delta q}(k)
\log_2
\frac{P_{q+\Delta q}(k)}{P_q(k)}.
\]

This quantity measures the instantaneous rate of change of the network's degree
structure as damage accumulates. To suppress high-frequency noise, the resulting
sequence is smoothed using an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA)
with fixed parameter $\alpha = 0.2$.

The smoothed signal,
\[
\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q),
\]
is the sole early-warning observable used throughout the study.

\subsection*{Early-Warning Criteria}
\label{sec:early_warning_criteria}
Rather than introducing multiple metrics, we apply regime-appropriate decision
rules to the same successive KL signal.

\subsubsection*{Random Failure: Baseline Deviation Rule}
Under random failure, networks exhibit an initial stationary regime in which
$\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q)$ fluctuates weakly around a constant baseline.
We define a baseline window
\[
q \le 0.15,
\]
and compute the baseline mean $\mu_0$ and standard deviation $\sigma_0$ over this range.

The early-warning point $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$ is defined as the smallest $q$ such that
\[
\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q) > \mu_0 + 2\sigma_0.
\]

This criterion detects the loss of structural stationarity without reference to
collapse thresholds or numerical derivatives.

\subsubsection*{Targeted Failure: Drift Rule on Midpoint Support}
Under targeted (hub-first) removal, structural change begins immediately and no
stationary baseline exists. Because successive KL is computed between adjacent damage
levels, we index the smoothed signal on midpoints
\[
q_{i+\frac12}=\frac{q_i+q_{i+1}}{2}.
\]
We define the warning point as the smallest midpoint $q_{i+\frac12}$ for which
$\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}$ exhibits persistent positive drift over $m$ increments, i.e.,
net increase over a window of length $m+1$ while allowing occasional small local
violations due to stochastic noise.


\paragraph{Implementation detail.}
Because $\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}$ is estimated from finite simulations, the drift criterion can be disrupted by occasional small downward fluctuations. In practice we therefore interpret ``persistent drift'' as requiring net increase over a window of $m$ increments, allowing at most a small number of local violations within that window, rather than demanding strict monotonicity at every step. This preserves the regime distinction (no stationary baseline under targeted removal) while reducing sensitivity to single-step noise.

\subsection*{Collapse Identification}
For reference only, we define the collapse point $q_{\mathrm{collapse}}$ as the smallest
$q$ such that
\[
S(q) < 0.1.
\]
This threshold is not used in the warning logic and serves solely to contextualize
warning lead times.

\paragraph{CAIDA operational collapse proxy (majority-loss).}
For the CAIDA AS graph only, we also report $q_{\mathrm{maj}}$, defined as the smallest
$q$ such that $S(q) < 0.5$, i.e., the point at which the giant connected component loses
majority status. This proxy is used only for interpretability and does not affect
warning detection.


\subsection*{Experimental Protocol}
All experiments use 100 evenly spaced values of $q \in [0, 0.9]$.
Each configuration is repeated across five random seeds.

For each value of $\gamma$ and each failure mode, we report the mean and standard
deviation of $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$ or $q_{\mathrm{trigger}}$ across seeds.

\subsection*{Empirical network: CAIDA AS graph (2026-01-01)}
We use the CAIDA AS Relationships Dataset (serial-2; snapshot 2026-01-01)~\cite{caida_as_rel_20260101}. We parse \texttt{AS1|AS2|rel|source}, drop comment lines, and form an undirected simple graph from unique AS pairs.

\subsection*{Datasets analyzed and where results appear}
This study reports results on two network classes:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Synthetic networks (primary analysis):} Chung--Lu graphs with power-law degree sequences, swept over $\gamma \in (2,3)$. These experiments are used to evaluate hypotheses H1--H3 and all detection-rule behavior under controlled structure.
    \item \textbf{Empirical network (validation):} the CAIDA AS Relationships graph (snapshot 2026-01-01). This serves as an out-of-sample realism check of the random-failure behavior observed in the synthetic setting.
\end{itemize}

Unless explicitly labeled as \emph{CAIDA}, all figures and tables in the Results section refer to the synthetic Chung--Lu experiments.

\section*{Analysis and Results}

We begin with a representative Chung--Lu example under random failure to illustrate the behavior
of $S(q)$ and the successive KL signal. We then present a random-failure case study on the CAIDA
AS graph as an empirical validation. Next, we report a representative targeted (hub-first) example
on synthetic Chung--Lu graphs to contrast behavior under adversarial damage. Finally, we summarize
the full $\gamma$ sweep by reporting tables of mean and standard deviation of warning/trigger and
collapse timings across seeds for both random and targeted removal.

We analyze the successive KL divergence under progressive node removal and evaluate its
effectiveness as an early-warning signal for structural collapse. For synthetic Chung--Lu
experiments, results are averaged across random seeds controlling both graph realization
and node removal. For CAIDA, the underlying graph is fixed and variability arises only from
the stochastic node-removal process; we report means and standard deviations across removal
seeds.

\subsection*{Synthetic (Chung--Lu) networks: Targeted Failure}

Under targeted (hub-first) removal, the successive KL signal departs immediately from any low-$q$ baseline-like regime observed under random failure.
The successive KL signal reflects immediate, nonstationary structural disruption, and its trend need not be monotone.

\subsection*{Analytical Contributions}

From the preceding analyses, we identify the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Rate-Based Early Warning: We demonstrate that the successive Kullback--Leibler divergence between degree distributions functions as an effective early-warning signal by capturing the rate of structural change, rather than relying on absolute connectivity or heterogeneity measures.

    \item Single Signal, Regime-Aware Interpretation: We show that the same information-theoretic signal can detect early warning under both random and targeted node removal, provided it is interpreted with regime-appropriate decision rules. This avoids the need for multiple ad hoc metrics while respecting fundamentally different failure dynamics.

    \item Robustness Across the Power-Law Regime: Through a sweep of degree exponents $2 < \gamma < 3$, we establish that early-warning behavior persists across heterogeneous scale-free networks, indicating that the signal is not an artifact of a specific network realization.

    \item Quantified Warning Lead Time: We empirically measure the separation between detected warning points and macroscopic collapse, showing that information-theoretic change rates provide nontrivial advance notice without reference to percolation thresholds.
\end{itemize}


\section*{Conclusion}

The present study focuses on node-based perturbations and the resulting
information-theoretic signatures of structural degradation in power-law
networks. While node removal is the classical setting for percolation
theory and robustness analysis, many real-world systems are equally, if
not more, vulnerable to the loss or corruption of edges rather
than nodes. Examples include communication links in the Internet backbone,
transmission lines in power grids, high-frequency transportation corridors,
and weighted or metadata-rich edges in social and biological networks.

Recent literature has noted that targeted dismantling of edges is far less
developed mathematically than node-based attacks, in part because the
importance of an edge depends jointly on the structural properties of the
two endpoints (Radicchi~et al.~2024). This creates a higher-dimensional
relationship that is not captured by node-centric percolation models. Given
that many empirical networks are weighted or possess edge-level attributes,
extending robustness analysis to the link domain remains an important open
direction.

A natural extension of the information-theoretic framework developed in this
work is to analyze how the edge distribution - and more generally the
joint distribution of endpoint features - changes under random and targeted
link removal. Analogues of the entropy $H(q)$ and KL divergence
$D_{KL}(D_q \,\|\, D_0)$ could be defined for edge-weight distributions,
edge-degree pairs $(k_i, k_j)$, or other edge-level metadata. Such
quantities may reveal early-warning signals of structural fragility that are
invisible to node-only analyses.

In future work, we plan to extend this information-theoretic methodology to
edge-based percolation and link attacks. This will require formalizing the
probability distributions over edge features and developing divergence-based
metrics that quantify how link-level structure degrades as the network
approaches its percolation threshold. Given the central role that edges play
in real infrastructure networks, this direction promises both theoretical
richness and practical relevance.
