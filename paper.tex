\begin{abstract}
Power-law networks tolerate extensive random failures yet collapse abruptly under targeted attacks with little warning from connectivity measures alone. We track smoothed successive Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence between adjacent empirical degree distributions in Chung--Lu (CL) graphs ($2<\gamma<3$) under progressive node removal using fixed detection rules that do not reference connectivity. Under random failure the signal rises above the calibration-window threshold before giant-component collapse (40/40 seeds at $\gamma=2.5$; replicated on an empirical Internet AS topology); under targeted removal it exhibits immediate disruption. The residual degree distribution is derived analytically via binomial thinning: a Fisher information approximation explains why the KL signal accelerates near the percolation threshold. Null and low-damage controls confirm specificity (0/40 false triggers in each). Successive KL produces earlier warning than rolling-variance early-warning signals under the same decision rule, a timing advantage attributable to rolling-window lag. Separately, the Fisher information $\mathcal{I}(q)$ of the analytical residual distribution grows near $q_c$, confirming pre-collapse signal acceleration analytically. The indicator's divergence between CL and the configuration model (CM) reveals its sensitivity to stub-pairing-induced structural correlations: the baseline KL noise floor is elevated in CM relative to CL ($\bar{\mu}_0$ by 1.4--3.8$\times$, $\bar{\sigma}_0$ by 2.0--6.1$\times$ across $\gamma$), inflating the detection threshold above the post-window signal in the majority of CM runs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Many real-world networks exhibit degree distributions of the form $P(k)\propto k^{-\gamma}$
with $\gamma\in(2,3)$~\citep{newman2003structure,barabasi1999scaling}, reflecting extreme
heterogeneity and the presence of high-degree hubs. Network robustness is traditionally
assessed via giant connected component (GCC) fraction $S(q)$ under node removal, which provides limited insight into
how structure degrades before fragmentation. Robustness transitions often occur abruptly
with little warning from classical connectivity indicators~\citep{radicchi2024robustness}.
\citet{suweis2014earlywarning} show that rising variance and
cross-correlation anticipate instability in node-state dynamics on networks; our setting
differs in that we monitor structural degradation under node removal with no node-state
time series, seeking early warning from topological reorganization via successive KL
divergence between degree distributions.

Information-theoretic quantities have characterized static graph structure but not progressive
damage. \citet{dehmer2008information} introduced Shannon-entropy measures for static
graph complexity; none connect entropy to node failures, percolation, or GCC collapse.
Robustness literature~\citep{albert2000error,cohen2000resilience,radicchi2024robustness}
relies on degree moments, spectral radius, or percolation thresholds, not on
information-theoretic indicators that change before collapse. KL divergence between successive degree distributions captures rates of structural
reorganization; the Fisher information of the residual distribution explains why the signal accelerates
near $q_c$. We ask whether such rates provide early-warning signals of impending
connectivity collapse in power-law networks under progressive node removal, and under what
conditions the signal transfers across generative ensembles with matched tail exponents.

Unlike entropy, which summarizes structure at a fixed damage level, successive KL divergence
measures rates of change; being sensitive to the full distributional shape rather than
low-order moments, it detects connectivity shifts before they appear macroscopically.
We use exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA)-smoothed successive KL divergence to detect the onset of nonstationary
structural reorganization under progressive node removal, using fixed decision rules that
do not reference connectivity; we do not estimate $q_c$, and in the targeted regime the
signal is interpreted as an immediate-disruption intensity summary rather than advance warning.
Across 40 independent seeds per $\gamma$ over nine values in $(2,3)$, the signal departs
from the calibration-window baseline at $q_{\mathrm{warn}} \ll q_{\mathrm{collapse}}$ in all or nearly
all seeds at every tested $\gamma$ (e.g., 40/40 at $\gamma=2.5$, median lead time
$\Delta_{\mathrm{warn}} \approx 0.55$), reproduced on a Center for Applied Internet Data Analysis (CAIDA) Autonomous System (AS) graph (40/40 seeds).
The residual degree distribution under random removal is derived analytically via binomial
thinning (Eq.~\ref{eq:binomial_thinning}), connecting $\tilde{P}(k;\,q)$ to the
Molloy--Reed threshold; a Fisher information approximation then shows that successive KL
per step is proportional to $\mathcal{I}(q)$, which grows as the high-degree tail contracts
near $q_c$, explaining the pre-collapse signal acceleration; empirically, the
baseline-deviation rule applied directly to successive KL produces earlier warning than
rolling-variance EWS under the same decision rule, a timing advantage attributable to
rolling-window lag rather than to $\mathcal{I}(q)$ acceleration.
Configuration-model experiments reveal that the indicator diverges between Chung--Lu and
configuration-model ensembles, highlighting its sensitivity to stub-pairing-induced structural
correlations that soft-constraint wiring does not introduce; the hard-constraint wiring
mechanism is an independent structural channel of ensemble sensitivity.

\section{Related Work}

Early-warning signals for tipping points have been studied across ecology, climate, finance, and other complex systems, where rising variance and lag-1 autocorrelation serve as generic indicators of critical slowing down near bifurcations \citep{scheffer2012anticipating,scheffer2009earlywarning}. These indicators are estimated from repeated observations of system state under a slowly drifting external parameter \citep{vannes2007slow,dakos2012methods}. Recent work extends EWS to machine-learning-based detection \citep{bury2021deeplearning} and to structural disintegration in complex systems via network-feature monitoring \citep{grassia2021machine}, as well as to percolation-based signals in coupled oscillatory systems \citep{ehstand2023network}. Our setting differs fundamentally: we have a single monotone structural-damage trajectory rather than a time series of repeated state observations, so variance and autocorrelation of a state variable are not available; instead we track successive KL divergence between adjacent empirical degree distributions, and we employ a fully analytical connection between this indicator and Fisher information.

Network resilience and robustness have been characterized analytically via degree-moment conditions \citep{molloy1995critical,callaway2000network} and percolation theory \citep{cohen2000resilience}. \citet{gao2016resilience} show that high-dimensional network dynamics can be reduced to a one-dimensional system, enabling resilience prediction from network structure; their framework targets dynamical resilience rather than structural early warning under damage. \citet{suweis2014earlywarning} demonstrate that rising variance and cross-correlation in node-state dynamics anticipate instability on networks, but rely on a time series of node states rather than the evolving degree distribution. The robustness literature \citep{albert2000error,radicchi2024robustness} relies primarily on GCC fraction and degree-moment thresholds, not information-theoretic quantities that change before collapse. Structural properties such as modularity further modulate robustness under degree-based and betweenness-based targeted attacks \citep{bellingeri2021modularity}, but the focus remains on post-collapse topology rather than precursor signals. Information-theoretic measures of static graph complexity were introduced by \citet{dehmer2008information} but do not address progressive damage or percolation. The present work applies successive KL divergence to the sequence of empirical degree distributions under node removal, connecting distributional change rates to the Fisher information of the binomial-thinning residual distribution.

\citet{lee2022degree} apply relative entropy to classify how the functional form of the residual degree distribution changes under sequential node removal across a spectrum of hub-protection strategies, parameterized by a continuous hub-preference exponent. Comparing the post-removal distribution against Poisson and power-law reference distributions, they identify a two-regime partition in the (removal fraction, hub-protection) parameter space, separating configurations where the distribution converges toward Poisson form from those retaining a power-law shape. Their central question is which reference distribution the post-removal snapshot most resembles; ours is whether the rate of successive distributional change provides advance warning of GCC collapse before it occurs. Both employ KL divergence as a structural diagnostic, but differ in reference distribution (fixed parametric forms versus the preceding empirical snapshot), in question (functional form classification versus early-warning detection), and in temporal framing (snapshot comparison versus continuous monitoring across the removal trajectory).

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Let $G_0$ have degree distribution $P_0(k)$. Progressive removal of fraction $q$ produces
damaged graph $G_q$ with distribution $P_q(k)$. We track the successive KL divergence:
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{q+\Delta q} \| P_q) = \sum_k P_{q+\Delta q}(k) \log_2 \frac{P_{q+\Delta
q}(k)}{P_q(k)}.
\]

We evaluate $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ as a per-step reorganization proxy, tracking warning timing
$q_{\mathrm{warn}}$, lead time $\Delta_{\mathrm{warn}}=q_{\mathrm{collapse}}-q_{\mathrm{warn}}$
(where $q_{\mathrm{collapse}}$ is defined by $S(q)<0.1$), and the robustness of these
statistics across the $\gamma$ sweep.


\subsection{Theoretical Background}

For uncorrelated random graphs with degree moments $\langle k \rangle$ and
$\langle k^2 \rangle$, the Molloy--Reed criterion~\citep{molloy1995critical,molloy1998size}
for GCC existence is
\[
\kappa = \frac{\langle k^2 \rangle - \langle k \rangle}{\langle k \rangle} > 1.
\]
Under independent random node failures with probability $q$, this gives
\[
(1 - q)\kappa = 1
\qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
q_c = 1 - \frac{\langle k \rangle}{\langle k^2 \rangle - \langle k \rangle}.
\]

Classical percolation theory thus characterizes collapse via low-order degree moments~\citep{dorogovtsev2008critical}.

Under random node removal, each surviving node of original degree $k$ retains each edge with probability $(1-q)$;
residual degree is Binomial$(k,\,1{-}q)$, giving
\begin{equation}
\tilde{P}(k';\,q)
\;=\;
\sum_{k \ge k'} P_0(k)\,\binom{k}{k'}(1-q)^{k'}\,q^{k-k'}.
\label{eq:binomial_thinning}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:binomial_thinning} is exact for locally tree-like graphs in the infinite-size limit~\citep{callaway2000network,cohen2000resilience} and provides an accurate approximation for finite Chung--Lu graphs at moderate damage levels.

The moments of $\tilde{P}$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
\langle k' \rangle = (1-q)\langle k \rangle_0,
\qquad
\langle k'^2 \rangle = (1-q)^2\langle k^2 \rangle_0 + (1-q)q\langle k \rangle_0,
\label{eq:thinned_moments}
\end{equation}
from which the Molloy--Reed branching factor under damage is
\[
\kappa(q)
= \frac{\langle k'^2 \rangle - \langle k' \rangle}{\langle k' \rangle}
= (1-q)\,\kappa_0,
\]
recovering the linear threshold condition $(1-q)\kappa_0 = 1$ stated above.

While $\kappa(q)$ evolves linearly in $q$, $\tilde{P}(k';\,q)$ undergoes nonlinear
reshaping: the tail contracts at rate $(1-q)^{k'}$, faster for large $k'$.
Substantial connectivity redistribution can thus occur while moments change only gradually.
Under random failure the tail erodes slowly; under targeted removal it distorts
immediately. Both processes can accumulate while $S(q)$ remains largely unchanged.
KL divergence, comparing full successive distributions, detects this redistribution where
moment-based indicators miss it. Our objective is not to predict $q_c$ but to detect the
onset of reorganization~\citep{radicchi2024robustness}.

For $q < q_c$, $\tilde{P}(k';\,q)$ is smooth (Eq.~\ref{eq:binomial_thinning}); for small $\Delta q$ (away from $q_c$, where tail contraction eventually invalidates the smooth-derivative assumption),
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\tilde{P}_{q+\Delta q}\,\|\,\tilde{P}_q\right)
\;\approx\;
\frac{(\Delta q)^2}{2}\,\mathcal{I}(q),
\qquad
\mathcal{I}(q)
=
\sum_k
\frac{\bigl[\partial\tilde{P}(k;\,q)/\partial q\bigr]^2}{\tilde{P}(k;\,q)},
\label{eq:fisher_approx}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{I}(q)$ is the Fisher information of $\tilde{P}$ with respect to $q$.
Near $q_c$, accelerated tail contraction drives $\mathcal{I}(q)$ upward, producing the
signal acceleration the detection rule captures. Under targeted failure, $\mathcal{I}(0)$
is already large; no calibration window forms.
The stability of the calibration window $q\le0.15$ in the empirical CL signal is an
empirical property visible in \autoref{fig:random_rep} and confirmed across window
choices in \autoref{tab:baseline_window_sensitivity}; stationarity here means no
sustained exceedances of $\mu_0+2\sigma_0$ within the window at the network scales and
degree exponents studied, not negligible absolute variance.

\subsection{Network Models}

We use Chung--Lu power-law graphs ($P(k)\propto k^{-\gamma}$, $2<\gamma<3$), a regime
characteristic of infrastructure and communication networks, with $N=10{,}000$ nodes fixed
across all synthetic experiments. Unlike CM,
which can exhibit degree correlations as a finite-size artifact, CL assigns edges
independently, motivating its use for the primary analysis.

We sweep $\gamma \in \{2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, 2.5, 2.6, 2.7, 2.8, 2.9\}$, averaging over
40 seeds per $\gamma$. We also analyze an empirical CAIDA AS graph and repeat the pipeline
on an exponent-matched CM ensemble (construction in the Appendix;
results in \S\ref{sec:config_model_check_results}).

\subsection{Failure Models}

Two failure regimes: (1) random, nodes removed uniformly at random; (2) targeted,
nodes removed in descending degree order. In both, $q \in [0, 0.9]$ of nodes are removed
and observables computed on $G_q$, implemented using NetworkX~\citep{SciPyProceedings_11}.

\subsection{Structural Signal: KL Divergence and Smoothing}

All signals derive from the empirical degree distribution
\[
P_q(k) = \frac{1}{|V(G_q)|} \sum_{v \in V(G_q)} \mathbf{1}\{\deg(v) = k\}.
\]
The successive KL divergence between adjacent levels is
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P_{q+\Delta q} \,\|\, P_q\right)
=
\sum_k
P_{q+\Delta q}(k)
\log_2
\frac{P_{q+\Delta q}(k)}{P_q(k)}.
\]
Large values indicate rapid reorganization; small values indicate stationary evolution.
Evaluated on a finite grid and EWMA-smoothed, we interpret it as a per-step change-rate,
not a continuous-time derivative. We also define degree entropy
\[
H(q) = -\sum_k P_q(k)\log_2 P_q(k),
\]
which summarizes heterogeneity at a fixed state. KL divergence detects reorganization even when $H(q)$ evolves gradually; entropy serves as a complementary indicator only.

\subsubsection*{Secondary control signals}
In the Controls section we also evaluate: (i) successive JS divergence~\citep{lin1991js},
\[
D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P_{q+\Delta q},P_q)=\tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{q+\Delta q}\|M)+\tfrac12
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_q\|M),
\qquad
M=\tfrac12(P_{q+\Delta q}+P_q),
\]
and (ii) absolute entropy change,
\[
|\Delta H(q)| = \big|H(q+\Delta q)-H(q)\big|.
\]
These assess specificity only.

We leave ordering-aware distances (e.g., Wasserstein on degree) for future comparator
extensions.

\subsubsection*{Moment-based comparator (Molloy--Reed branching factor)}
We also compute $\kappa(q)=(\langle k^2\rangle_q-\langle k\rangle_q)/\langle k\rangle_q$
from empirical $P_q(k)$, with the same EWMA and detection rule; full results are reported in the Appendix.

\subsubsection*{Support and numerical stability}
Degree distributions are evaluated on a fixed discrete support with additive
regularization $\varepsilon = 10^{-12}$ before normalization. EWMA smoothing
suppresses finite-size fluctuations:
\[
\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_i)
=
\alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_i)
+
(1-\alpha)\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{i-1}).
\]
$\alpha=0.2$ is fixed across all experiments. The smoothed signal $\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q)$ is
the sole warning observable; alternatives appear only in controls.

\subsection{Decision Rules}
\phantomsection
\label{sec:early_warning_criteria}
Two regime-appropriate rules apply to the same signal.

\subsubsection{Random Failure: Baseline Deviation Rule}
The window boundary $q_0 = 0.15$ is chosen empirically; sensitivity to this choice is
reported in \autoref{tab:baseline_window_sensitivity}, which shows detection rates of
39--40/40 across $q_0 \in \{0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.25\}$ at all tested $\gamma$
(\S\ref{sec:baseline_window_sensitivity}).
We estimate $\mu_0$ and $\sigma_0$ over the window $q \le 0.15$,
which contains $\approx16$ midpoints on the 100-step grid (step size $\Delta q=0.009$,
midpoints at $(i-0.5)\cdot0.009$ for $i=1,\ldots,16$).
The window is fixed across all $\gamma$ and not tuned post hoc.

The early-warning point $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$ is defined as the smallest $q$ such that
\[
\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q) > \mu_0 + 2\sigma_0.
\]

Non-detections are recorded as NaN; detection rates $[n_{\mathrm{det}}/n]$ and
median [IQR] are reported over seeds where $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$ is defined.

\subsubsection{Targeted Failure: Intensity Summary (no timing estimand)}
Under targeted removal, disruption begins immediately; the signal does not admit a
calibration window. KL is evaluated at midpoints, so the earliest reportable value is
$q_{1/2}=\Delta q/2$, at which the response triggers; timing is not a meaningful estimand.
We summarize targeted disruption by the initial shock magnitude
\[
I_{\mathrm{tgt}} := \tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{1/2}),
\]
reported with $q_{\mathrm{collapse}}$ (defined by $S(q)<0.1$), avoiding
overinterpretation of timing. The collapse proxy is post hoc only; changing its threshold
affects only reported lead times.

\subsection{Experimental Design}
Random failure uses a 100-point grid over $[0,0.9]$; targeted removal uses 400 points.
Each configuration is repeated across 40 seeds. Each seed generates an independent Chung--Lu
graph realization and an independent random node-removal order, so the ensemble averages
over both network heterogeneity and removal stochasticity.
CAIDA experiments use $q_{\max}=0.99$ with 200 points.

We use the CAIDA AS Relationships Dataset (serial-2; snapshot
\CAIDASnapshot)~\citep{caida_as_rel_20260101}. We parse
\texttt{AS1|AS2|rel|source}, drop comment lines, and form an undirected simple graph from
unique AS pairs.

Three network classes: (1) \textbf{Chung--Lu} (primary), $\gamma \in (2,3)$, both
failure regimes, reporting $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$ and $I_{\mathrm{tgt}}$; (2)
\textbf{CAIDA AS graph} (\CAIDASnapshot), out-of-sample realism check; (3)
\textbf{configuration-model} ensembles: (a) exponent-matched (same $\gamma$, fresh degree
sequence) and (b) sequence-matched (exact realized CL degree sequence), isolating the
hard-constraint wiring mechanism. Unless labeled otherwise, all figures and tables refer
to Chung--Lu.

\subsection{Degree-Class Structure of the KL Signal}
\label{sec:kl_degree_structure}
To show which degree classes drive $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$, we define the per-degree contribution
$c(k;\,q) = P_{q+\Delta q}(k)\log_2[P_{q+\Delta q}(k)/P_q(k)]$,
so that $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{q+\Delta q}\|P_q) = \sum_k c(k;\,q)$.
Figure~\ref{fig:kl_lorenz} plots the cumulative fraction of $\sum_k|c(k;\,q)|$ against
degree percentile (degrees sorted ascending) at a representative post-warning snapshot
($q=0.30$, which lies just above the median $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$ for all three $\gamma$ values shown;
Table~\ref{tab:gamma_sweep_random}).
All three curves lie far above the diagonal: the bottom 50\% of occupied degree classes
($P_q(k)>0$) account for 94--99\% of $\sum_k|c(k;\,q)|$ at $q=0.30$, consistent across 10 seeds.
The signal is therefore concentrated in the low-$k$ classes, not the high-$k$ tail where hubs reside.
This is mechanistically expected: when any node is removed under random failure, each neighbour loses one link and shifts one step left in the degree distribution.
Because $P(k)\sim k^{-\gamma}$ places the overwhelming majority of nodes at low degrees, these shifts produce large absolute changes in $P(k)$ at small $k$ and negligible changes at large $k$ where $P(k)\approx 0$.
The KL signal therefore tracks diffuse low-degree redistribution in the surviving subgraph, not isolated hub-removal events.

\subsection{Rolling EWS Comparators}
\label{sec:ews_comparators}
Two rolling indicators, both under the identical detection rule
($\alpha=0.20$, $z=2$, $q_0\le0.15$):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rolling standard deviation} $\mathrm{sd}_w(D_{\mathrm{KL}})$: the sample
    standard deviation of the raw successive KL values within a sliding window of $w=7$
    steps ($\Delta q \approx 0.063$ of damage fraction). Rising variance is a classical
    signature of critical fluctuations near a tipping point~\citep{dakos2012methods}.
    \item \textbf{Rolling lag-1 autocorrelation} $\mathrm{AC}(1)_w(D_{\mathrm{KL}})$: the
    Pearson correlation between successive KL values at lag 1 within the same sliding window.
    Rising autocorrelation (critical slowing down) is a second standard EWS
    indicator~\citep{dakos2012methods,vannes2007slow}.
\end{itemize}
\autoref{tab:ews_comparison} summarizes detection rates and median $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$
[IQR] across 40 seeds for $\gamma \in \{2.5, 2.7, 3.0\}$. $\gamma=3.0$ is included here
as an out-of-range test point (beyond the main sweep of $\gamma\in(2,3)$) to probe
comparator behavior near the lighter-tail boundary; it is absent from the primary CL
ensemble tables, targeted removal, and noise-floor comparison, which are restricted
to $\gamma\in\{2.1,\ldots,2.9\}$.
Rolling-sd matches detection rate (40/40 at $\gamma=2.5$, 39/40 at $\gamma=3.0$) but
$q_{\mathrm{warn}}$ is $\approx0.013$--$0.023$ later, reflecting rolling-window lag.
Rolling-AC(1): 22/40 ($\gamma=2.5$), 15/40 ($\gamma=2.7$), 18/40 ($\gamma=3.0$), and
consistently later $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$. The low AC(1) rate reflects the short effective
memory of the KL sequence relative to the rolling window. Direct baseline deviation on $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ warns earlier (lower median $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$)
and with tighter spread (lower IQR) than both rolling-window alternatives at every $\gamma$;
it matches rolling-SD on detection rate and substantially outperforms rolling-AC(1) (40/40 vs 22/40 at $\gamma=2.5$).

\subsection{Fisher Information Shape: Mean-Field Reference}
\label{sec:analytical_kl}

Supplementary Fig.~S1 shows $\mathcal{I}(q)$, computed numerically from
$\tilde{P}(k';\,q)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:binomial_thinning}) via central finite differences,
for $\gamma\in\{2.1,2.5,2.9\}$ on a log scale.

$\mathcal{I}(q)$ is large at small $q$, declines through a mid-damage minimum near
$q\approx0.4$--$0.5$, then grows again toward $q_c$; successive KL per step scales as
$(\Delta q)^2\,\mathcal{I}(q)/2$, so the post-minimum rise in successive KL remains small
in absolute terms. For $\gamma=2.1$ and $\gamma=2.5$, $q_c$ lies beyond $q_{\max}=0.9$
and the near-$q_c$ uptick is not visible in the figure; for $\gamma=2.9$, $q_c\approx0.80$
falls within the plotted range. Here $q_c$ denotes the Molloy--Reed percolation threshold
applied to the population-level residual distribution $\tilde{P}(k';\,q)$; the empirical
collapse fraction $q_{\mathrm{collapse}}$ (defined by $S(q)<0.1$ in finite graphs)
differs from this analytical threshold due to finite-size effects and graph
stochasticity---see \autoref{tab:gamma_sweep_random} for empirical values.

\autoref{fig:analytical_kl} shows the analytical $\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q)$ after EWMA
smoothing ($\alpha=0.20$) alongside the detection threshold $\mu_0+2\sigma_0$ for each
$\gamma$.
In every panel the threshold (blue dashed) sits above the post-window signal throughout
$q>0.15$, and the baseline-deviation rule produces no detection ($q_{\mathrm{warn}}=\mathrm{none}$
for all $\gamma\in\{2.1,2.3,2.5,2.7,2.9\}$).
The mechanism is visible directly: the calibration window $q\le0.15$ falls in the
high-amplitude early regime of $\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q)$, so $\mu_0$ is estimated from
large values and the resulting threshold clears the post-window signal by a substantial margin.

The calibration window $q \le 0.15$ falls in the high-$\mathcal{I}(q)$ early regime.
In the empirical CL signal, the calibration window exhibits lower amplitude and variance than
this analytical early regime. We hypothesize that simultaneous stub deletion, which removes
all edges incident to a deleted node at once rather than independently, induces correlated
neighbourhood updates absent from binomial thinning and suppresses the early empirical
baseline below the population-level prediction. Near $q_c$, cascade-induced correlated shifts
lift the empirical signal above the mean-field prediction. The post-minimum monotone rise in $\mathcal{I}(q)$ confirms
that distributional change accelerates approaching $q_c$.

\subsection{Degree-Matched Configuration-Model Diagnostic}
\label{sec:degree_matched_config}
The existing configuration-model experiment (\nameref{sec:config_model_check_results}) matches
$\gamma$ between the Chung--Lu source and the configuration-model network, but uses an
independently sampled power-law degree sequence for each CM run.
To sharpen the mechanistic attribution, we introduce a degree-matched variant:
for each (gamma, CL seed) pair we (1) generate a Chung--Lu graph $G_{\mathrm{CL}}$ and extract
its realized degree sequence $\mathbf{d}$, then (2) construct 5 CM realizations from
that exact degree sequence, varying only the stub-pairing randomness.
With 10 CL seeds per $\gamma$, this yields 50 sequence-matched CM runs per $\gamma$.
This controls for the degree sequence itself; any residual detection difference isolates the
effect of the hard-constraint wiring mechanism from distributional properties of $\mathbf{d}$.

\autoref{tab:degree_matched_config} summarizes detection rates across $\gamma$ values.
Detection rates in the sequence-matched CM are intermediate between those of the CL ensemble
(40/40 at all tested $\gamma$) and the exponent-matched CM ($\approx19$\% detection rate
from the main ensemble experiment): at $\gamma=2.5$, 28/50 sequence-matched CM runs
produce a valid warning, compared to $\approx$4/40 in the exponent-matched CM.
This pattern implies that both the degree sequence and the wiring mechanism
contribute to detection behavior: when the degree sequence is drawn from the same CL
source, detection improves substantially, but the hard-constraint wiring still elevates the noise
floor relative to CL. The residual gap ($28/50$ vs $40/40$) confirms that stub-pairing
randomness, independent of the degree sequence, is a distinct structural factor shaping
the indicator's behavior.

Together, the two experiments decompose ensemble sensitivity into two separable
causes: hard-constraint wiring and degree sequence realization.
At $\gamma=2.5$, the gap between sequence-matched CM (28/50) and CL (40/40) isolates hard-constraint wiring: stub-pairing
elevates calibration-window churn even when the degree sequence is identical.
The further gap between exponent-matched CM ($\approx$4/40) and sequence-matched CM (28/50)
isolates degree sequence realization: drawing a fresh sequence at each seed adds a second source of noise floor
elevation on top of the wiring effect.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion_noise_floor}

Across Chung--Lu power-law graphs with $2<\gamma<3$, EWMA-smoothed successive KL divergence
between adjacent empirical degree distributions departs from baseline after the calibration
window before GCC collapse under random node removal, yielding $q_{\mathrm{warn}} \ll
q_{\mathrm{collapse}}$ in all or nearly all seeds at every tested $\gamma$; this early warning success extends to the CAIDA
AS graph. Under targeted (hub-first) removal, no calibration window exists; we instead record
initial shock magnitude $I_{\mathrm{tgt}} := \tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{1/2})$. Controls
confirm specificity: successive KL produces zero false triggers in null and low-damage regimes
whereas entropy-change and rolling-sd signals trigger frequently (37/40 and 27/40). Successive KL 
divergence fires earlier than rolling-sd EWS by 0.013--0.027 in median $q_{\mathrm{warn}}$ across 
tested $\gamma$ (\autoref{tab:ews_comparison}), and detects more consistently than rolling AC(1) (39--40/40 vs.\ 15--22/40).
The degree-class decomposition (Section~\ref{sec:kl_degree_structure}) shows that the signal tracks diffuse low-degree redistribution rather than hub-removal events.

Signal acceleration near $q_c$ is explained analytically via binomial thinning: the
Fisher information $\mathcal{I}(q)$ of the residual distribution grows as the high-degree
tail contracts near the percolation threshold, producing $(\Delta q)^2\,\mathcal{I}(q)$
per-step KL that accelerates approaching $q_c$, matching the observed post-window rise.
This analytical result characterises signal behaviour near $q_c$ only; it does not predict
calibration window stability.
Calibration window stability is an empirical property, validated here at $N=10{,}000$
across $\gamma\in(2,3)$: visible in \autoref{fig:random_rep}, robust to window-endpoint
choice (\autoref{tab:baseline_window_sensitivity}), and confirmed by the null and
low-damage controls (0/40 false triggers in each), but not derived from the
binomial-thinning framework, and its behaviour at other network scales or degree
distributions remains an open empirical question.

The indicator's divergence between Chung--Lu and the configuration model reveals the
indicator's sensitivity to localized structural correlations and finite-size stub-matching
constraints that mean-field models ignore.
The key observable governing ensemble transfer is the baseline KL noise floor.
In Chung--Lu graphs, the expected-degree wiring (soft degree constraint) produces
smooth, low-amplitude step-to-step variation in $P_q(k)$: at $\gamma=2.5$,
$\bar{\mu}_0=0.058$ and $\bar{\sigma}_0=0.011$ (\autoref{tab:baseline_noise_comparison}), so the
threshold $\bar{\mu}_0+2\bar{\sigma}_0=0.080$ lies below the post-window signal in all 40 seeds
(detection rate 40/40). Configuration-model graphs (hard degree constraint) exhibit substantially
higher baseline noise: $\bar{\mu}_0=0.118$ and $\bar{\sigma}_0=0.032$
(\autoref{tab:baseline_noise_comparison}), driving the threshold to $0.182$, consistent with
36/40 non-detecting seeds ($n_{\mathrm{det}}=4/40$, \autoref{tab:baseline_noise_comparison}).
The mechanistic source is stub-pairing randomness at construction time: hard-constraint
wiring introduces degree--degree correlations and local clustering into the initial CM graph
that are absent under CL's soft expected-degree constraint, and these baked-in correlations
produce elevated step-to-step variability in $P_q(k)$ as nodes are removed from the 
graph. This pattern holds across $\gamma$: CM detection rates range from 1/40 to
14/40 for every $\gamma\in\{2.1,\ldots,2.9\}$ (\autoref{tab:baseline_noise_comparison}),
while CL detects in all or nearly all seeds at every $\gamma$.

The Fisher information $\mathcal{I}(q)$ of the binomial-thinning residual distribution provides a population-level reference for the KL signal's shape across the damage trajectory.
$\mathcal{I}(q)$ computed from binomial thinning (supplementary Fig.~S1) is large at
small $q$, reaches a minimum in the mid-damage range, then grows toward $q_c$; since
successive KL per step scales as $(\Delta q)^2\mathcal{I}(q)/2$, the post-minimum rise
remains small in absolute terms. The calibration window $q\le0.15$ falls on the high-$\mathcal{I}(q)$ early portion of the
analytical curve, so the population-level signal is not intrinsically quiet there.
The empirical signal's stability during $q\le0.15$ in finite CL graphs is not fully
explained by the binomial-thinning model; we hypothesize that simultaneous stub deletion,
which removes all edges incident to a deleted node at once rather than independently,
induces correlated neighbourhood updates that suppress the early empirical baseline below
the population-level prediction.
Characterizing this mechanism analytically and testing its $N$-dependence remains future work.
Under the same baseline-deviation rule applied to the analytical signal, detection does not occur
(\autoref{fig:analytical_kl}): the calibration window $q \le 0.15$ falls on the elevated early
portion of $\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q)$, so $\mu_0$ and $\sigma_0$ are estimated from a
high-amplitude region, placing the threshold above the post-window signal at every $\gamma$.
Empirical detection in the Chung--Lu ensemble succeeds because correlated deletion
suppresses the early empirical baseline below the population-level prediction, giving the
post-window rise room to exceed the threshold; detection depends on this structural mechanism,
not on population-level dynamics alone. The binomial-thinning model (Eq.~\ref{eq:binomial_thinning}) assumes each edge
survives independently with probability $(1-q)$, whereas actual node removal deletes all
edges incident to a removed node at once. We hypothesize that this correlated deletion
keeps the empirical baseline lower and flatter than the population-level prediction,
concentrating distributional shift into the post-window signal; the analytical framework
correctly characterizes population-level dynamics near $q_c$ but does not capture this
mechanism. This identifies a scope condition for the baseline-deviation rule: the calibration
window must fall in a low-noise region of the empirical signal. For the Chung--Lu graphs studied here, $q \le 0.15$ satisfies this condition
empirically; the population-level $\mathcal{I}(q)$ analysis identifies ensemble type
and network scale as the structural parameters governing its transferability.

The CL--CM performance gap is quantifiable as a signal-to-noise ratio.
The SNR contrast itself encodes structural information:
$\mathrm{SNR}=\max_{q>q_0}\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q)\,/\,(\mu_0+z\sigma_0)$ exceeds one
in all CL seeds (detection rate 40/40 at every $\gamma$) and falls below one in 26--39/40
CM seeds at every $\gamma$ (\autoref{tab:baseline_noise_comparison}, $n_{\mathrm{det}}$ column),
quantifying exactly how much of the indicator's dynamic range is consumed by stub-pairing
correlations rather than damage-driven reorganization. Pre-estimating $\mu_0$ and $\sigma_0$ from ensemble
structure or a reference run, rather than from the damage window, is the natural path to
ensemble-stable transfer.

The absence of a KL precursor under targeted hub removal is consistent with Cohen et
al.~\citep{cohen2001breakdown}: scale-free networks with $\gamma \leq 3$ collapse after
eliminating only a vanishingly small fraction of hubs, so the degree distribution is
distorted at the first removal step and no calibration window forms. The result
motivates a clean separation between two monitoring objectives: (i) advance warning of
collapse under gradual random attrition, and (ii) shock quantification under targeted or
adversarial removal via $I_{\mathrm{tgt}}$.

The temporal advantage of the smoothed successive KL divergence signal over classical rolling-variance metrics stems from a fundamental difference in how they process structural data.
Variance collapses a complex distribution into a single scalar statistic, losing information about distributional shape; KL divergence is sensitive to shape changes across the entire empirical degree spectrum.
By directly measuring the information distance between successive steps, the metric bypasses the inherent lag of sliding-window estimators.
The divergence between Chung--Lu and configuration-model signatures further demonstrates that the signal is sensitive to structural correlations embedded in the wiring mechanics of the graph ensemble, independent of the degree exponent $\gamma$.

This study intentionally restricts the structural state to the empirical degree distribution
to keep the signal interpretable and the decision rule fixed across regimes; richer summaries
(joint-degree structure, mixing, motifs) may refine warning behavior under targeted removal.
All synthetic experiments use $N=10{,}000$ nodes; the CAIDA AS graph ($N=78{,}673$) provides one cross-scale data point (40-seed
detection rate 40/40 with qualitatively matched signal shape), though systematic
finite-size analysis remains future work.
Natural next steps are to track change rates in higher-order structural features, to test
analogous rules under link removal and correlated or temporally clustered failure processes,
and to extend the analysis to hub-protecting removal strategies in which lower-fitness or
lower-degree nodes are preferentially removed \citep{lee2022degree}.

\subsection*{S5. Configuration-Model Graph Construction Details}
\label{sec:config_model_check}
Detection results and mechanistic interpretation for the exponent-matched configuration-model
ensemble experiment are reported in the main Results section.
Construction details are provided here.

\subsubsection*{Exponent-matched CM (main experiment)}
A target degree sequence is drawn from a discrete power-law distribution with exponent $\gamma$
and minimum degree $k_{\min}=1$, truncated to $N=10{,}000$ nodes. The sequence is passed to the
configuration-model stub-matching procedure; self-loops and parallel edges are removed; and the
experiment is conducted on the largest connected component. Each ($\gamma$, seed) run uses an
independently sampled degree sequence.

\subsubsection*{Sequence-matched CM (degree-matched diagnostic)}
For each ($\gamma$, cl\_seed) pair, a Chung--Lu graph $G_{\mathrm{CL}}$ is generated and its
realized degree sequence $\mathbf{d}$ is extracted. Multiple CM wiring replicates are then
constructed from $\mathbf{d}$ (varying only the stub-pairing seed), producing CM graphs with
the exact same degree sequence as the CL source. Self-loops and parallel edges are removed and
the experiment is conducted on the largest connected component.
This design controls for the degree sequence and isolates the effect of the hard-constraint
wiring mechanism.
